[Virtual asset](/blog/outsourcing-and-third-party-risk-for-virtual-asset-firms/) operators navigating the evolving regulatory landscape face complex decisions about where to establish and grow their businesses. Jurisdictions like Dubai have moved quickly to create comprehensive frameworks, attracting significant investment and innovation in the digital asset space. Understanding these established models provides valuable context for firms considering new markets.

In Pakistan, the proposed Pakistan [Virtual Assets](/blog/how-pakistani-courts-have-treated-virtual-assets-so-far/) Regulatory Authority (PVARA) framework aims to bring clarity and oversight to the sector. For operators, comparing this emerging framework with a mature one like Dubai's [Virtual Asset Regulatory](/blog/how-to-respond-to-a-regulatory-consultation-and-why-it-matters/) Authority (VARA) offers critical insights into potential operational requirements, market dynamics, and the overall regulatory philosophy. This comparison is not just academic; it directly impacts strategic planning, compliance costs, and market access for any firm looking to operate in either jurisdiction.

This analysis focuses on the differences in licensing, regulatory scope, and compliance expectations, providing a practical guide for businesses. It helps operators anticipate the unique challenges and opportunities presented by Pakistan's developing [virtual asset](/blog/p2p-trading-platforms-and-the-regulatory-perimeter/) ecosystem, especially as the PVARA framework moves towards finalisation.

## What are PVARA and VARA Dubai?

PVARA, or the Pakistan Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority, is the proposed regulatory body in Pakistan intended to oversee and license [virtual asset](/blog/stablecoins-under-pakistani-regulation-how-are-they-treated/) activities. VARA, the [Virtual Asset](/blog/nfts-and-virtual-asset-rules-when-does-an-nft-become-regulated/) Regulatory Authority, is the established regulator for [virtual asset](/blog/the-compliance-officer-role-what-regulators-expect-from-an-mlro/) services within Dubai, specifically operating under the Dubai World Trade Centre Authority (DWTCA) Free Zone. PVARA is currently at the consultation and development stage, while VARA is fully operational with a comprehensive rulebook.

The establishment of a dedicated authority like PVARA reflects Pakistan's commitment to addressing the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) recommendations on virtual assets. The Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan (SECP) and the [State Bank](/blog/the-state-bank-of-pakistans-position-on-virtual-assets-explained/) of Pakistan (SBP) have been instrumental in the foundational work, but PVARA is envisioned as the primary, standalone regulator. Similarly, VARA was created to provide regulatory certainty and foster innovation within Dubai's specific economic zone, making it a distinct entity from the broader UAE federal financial regulators.

## Who do these regulations apply to?

Both PVARA and VARA Dubai aim to regulate Virtual Asset Service Providers (VASPs), which include entities offering services such as exchange, transfer, custody, and issuance of virtual assets. The scope of regulated activities under both frameworks is broad, encompassing most commercial operations involving virtual assets, but specific definitions and exemptions may vary. Understanding who needs a VASP licence in Pakistan and who does not is crucial for businesses.

For PVARA, the intention is to cover any entity facilitating virtual asset transactions for customers within Pakistan. This includes virtual asset exchanges, custodians, and potentially issuers of certain types of virtual assets. VARA's remit in Dubai extends to any entity conducting virtual asset activities that involve customers or operations within its jurisdiction, requiring a licence for a comprehensive list of services including advisory, brokerage, exchange, lending, and payments. The key is that both frameworks seek to capture the full spectrum of commercial virtual asset services to ensure market integrity and consumer protection.

## What are the key differences in regulatory approach?

The primary difference in regulatory approach lies in their stage of development and the underlying economic context. PVARA is being designed within a nascent regulatory environment for virtual assets in Pakistan, heavily influenced by global anti-money laundering (AML) and combating the financing of terrorism (CFT) standards, particularly FATF Recommendation 15. VARA, conversely, operates in a more established financial hub with a proactive stance on digital innovation, aiming to attract global crypto businesses through a clear and comprehensive framework.

VARA's approach has been characterised by a staged licensing process, starting with provisional permits and minimum viable product (MVP) licences before full market product (FMP) licences. This allows for controlled growth and learning. PVARA's proposed framework, while also focusing on robust AML/CFT controls, is likely to prioritise establishing foundational oversight and consumer protection measures from the outset, given Pakistan's unique economic challenges and the SBP's historical stance on virtual assets. ## How do licensing categories compare?

Both PVARA and VARA Dubai define different licensing categories based on the specific virtual asset services offered, but the granularity and terminology differ. VARA has a detailed classification system, while PVARA's proposed categories are still under development but are expected to align with common international standards for VASP services.

VARA Dubai categorises licences based on the type of virtual asset activity, such as advisory, broker-dealer, exchange, lending and borrowing, payments and remittances, and management and investment services. Each category has distinct requirements. PVARA is expected to introduce categories for services like virtual asset exchange, virtual asset custody, and potentially virtual asset issuance, reflecting the core activities identified by FATF. Our article on PVARA licence categories explained provides more detail on the anticipated structure. The specific requirements for each category, including capital requirements for virtual asset firms, will be a critical area of comparison once PVARA's rules are finalised.

## What are the main compliance obligations?

Both PVARA and VARA Dubai impose stringent compliance obligations on licensed VASPs, particularly concerning Anti-Money Laundering (AML), Combating the Financing of Terrorism (CFT), and consumer protection. However, the specific reporting frequencies, data requirements, and emphasis on certain aspects may vary due to differing local contexts and regulatory priorities.

VARA's compliance framework includes detailed requirements for customer due diligence (CDD), transaction monitoring, suspicious transaction reporting, cybersecurity, and investor protection mechanisms, including client asset segregation and complaints handling. PVARA's framework will also mandate robust AML/CFT controls, aligning with FATF standards, which means requirements for customer due diligence for crypto exchanges and understanding suspicious transaction reports will be central. Additionally, PVARA is expected to impose strict rules on record keeping obligations and business continuity planning for VASPs. Market coverage from [CoinConnect](https://coinconnect.site) notes that most firms underestimate how long the corporate structuring stage takes for new virtual asset businesses in emerging markets, often overlooking the detailed compliance planning required from day one.

Here is a summary of typical compliance areas:

*   **Anti-Money Laundering (AML) & Combating the Financing of Terrorism (CFT):** Both regulators require comprehensive AML/CFT programmes, including Know Your Customer (KYC) and CDD procedures, transaction monitoring, and suspicious transaction reporting. Pakistan's emphasis on FATF recommendations means this area will be particularly robust under PVARA.
*   **Capital Requirements:** VASPs must maintain adequate capital to cover operational risks and protect client funds. The specific amounts and types of eligible capital will differ significantly between the jurisdictions.
*   **Custody of Client Assets:** Rules for segregating client virtual assets from the firm's own assets are standard. Our article on custody rules explains how client virtual assets must be segregated.
*   **Cybersecurity:** Robust cybersecurity frameworks are mandatory to protect against hacks and data breaches.
*   **Data Protection:** Compliance with local data protection laws is essential for both.
*   **Regulatory Reporting:** Regular reporting of financial, operational, and compliance data is required. The VASP regulatory reporting calendar in Pakistan will outline these obligations.
*   **Fit and Proper Tests:** Key personnel, including directors and senior management, must undergo fit and proper tests. This ensures that individuals managing the VASP are competent and of good standing, as detailed in our guide on fit and proper tests for crypto licence directors.
*   **Travel Rule:** Both jurisdictions are expected to implement FATF's Travel Rule, requiring VASPs to share originator and beneficiary information for virtual asset transfers above certain thresholds.

## How do the enforcement powers differ?

While the specifics of PVARA's enforcement powers are still being finalised, they are anticipated to include a range of measures similar to those available to established financial regulators globally. VARA Dubai already possesses comprehensive enforcement capabilities, reflecting its operational status and the maturity of its regulatory framework.

VARA's enforcement toolkit includes issuing warnings, imposing fines, restricting or suspending licences, and ultimately revoking licences for non-compliance. They also have powers to conduct investigations and demand information. PVARA's enforcement powers are likely to mirror these, enabling the authority to ensure compliance with its rules, impose penalties for breaches, and take action against unlicensed operators. This includes powers related to monitoring and surveillance, as well as the ability to issue directives and conduct audits. ## What are the implications for operators?

For virtual asset operators, the comparison reveals distinct strategic considerations. Dubai offers a clear, established pathway for licensing and operation, with a regulatory environment designed to foster innovation and attract international businesses. This comes with high compliance standards and significant capital requirements, but also access to a mature market and a robust ecosystem.

Pakistan, on the other hand, presents an emerging market with significant growth potential, but also a developing regulatory framework. Operators considering Pakistan must be prepared for a dynamic regulatory environment, potentially evolving rules, and a strong emphasis on AML/CFT compliance driven by national and international imperatives. The process of obtaining a licence will require careful attention to detail, a thorough understanding of the proposed PVARA rules, and a commitment to ongoing compliance. Businesses should also consider the broader regulatory landscape, including the role of the FBR in treating crypto gains and the SECP's involvement in company law for virtual asset firms.
