For virtual asset service providers (VASPs), operating across different countries often means navigating a complex web of distinct regulatory requirements. Each jurisdiction typically demands its own licence, a process that can be costly, time-consuming, and resource-intensive. This fragmentation creates significant hurdles for businesses aiming for global reach or even regional expansion.

The ideal scenario for many operators would be a system where a licence obtained in one jurisdiction is automatically recognised, or "passported," into another. Such a mechanism could streamline operations, reduce compliance burdens, and foster greater innovation by lowering barriers to entry for legitimate businesses.

As Pakistan's virtual asset regulatory framework continues to develop under the Pakistan Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority (PVARA), understanding the potential for cross-border recognition and licence passporting becomes crucial for both local and international operators considering the Pakistani market.

## What is crypto licence passporting?

Crypto licence passporting refers to a regulatory mechanism that allows a virtual asset business, licensed in one jurisdiction, to operate in other participating jurisdictions without needing to obtain a separate, full licence in each additional country. It simplifies market access by creating a unified regulatory space.

This concept is well-established in traditional financial services, particularly within economic blocs like the European Union. For virtual assets, it aims to reduce duplication of regulatory effort and compliance costs. Instead of applying for a new licence in every country, a VASP would leverage its existing approval, subject to specific conditions and potentially simplified notification processes. This typically requires a high degree of regulatory alignment and mutual trust between the participating jurisdictions regarding their respective oversight standards and enforcement capabilities.

## Why is cross-border recognition important for VASPs?

Cross-border recognition is vital for VASPs because it enables scalable growth, reduces operational complexity, and fosters a more interconnected global virtual asset ecosystem. Without it, businesses face significant barriers to expansion.

The current landscape of fragmented regulations means that a VASP wishing to serve customers in multiple countries must often undergo separate licensing processes, each with its unique application requirements, capital demands, and ongoing compliance obligations. This can lead to increased legal and operational costs, slower market entry, and a higher risk of regulatory non-compliance across different jurisdictions. Effective cross-border recognition could unlock new markets, facilitate international transfers, and ultimately benefit consumers through increased competition and innovation. It is also a key factor in how a foreign exchange might legally serve Pakistani users, which is a complex area.

## How does Pakistan's PVARA approach this concept?

PVARA, as the prospective primary regulator for virtual assets in Pakistan, is currently developing its framework, which is expected to consider international best practices, including mechanisms for cross-border recognition. The authority's approach will likely be shaped by Pakistan's commitments to international standards set by bodies like the Financial Action Task Force (FATF).

While specific passporting rules are not yet finalised, PVARA's regulatory framework, currently in consultation, is anticipated to establish robust standards for anti-money laundering (AML) and combating the financing of terrorism (CFT), consumer protection, and operational resilience. These foundational elements are critical prerequisites for any future discussions on mutual recognition with other jurisdictions. Operators should monitor [PVARA's official website](https://pvara.org) for updates on these developments. The authority's stance will be crucial in defining who needs a VASP licence in Pakistan and who does not, particularly for entities with an international presence.

## What are the international precedents for passporting?

International precedents for passporting typically involve either a full mutual recognition agreement or a harmonisation of regulatory standards across multiple jurisdictions, often within a single economic bloc. The European Union's Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) regulation is a prominent example.

MiCA aims to create a unified framework for crypto-assets across all EU member states. Once fully implemented, a VASP authorised in one EU country under MiCA will be able to provide its services across the entire EU bloc, subject to notification requirements. This represents a significant step towards true passporting for virtual asset services. Other jurisdictions, such as Singapore and Dubai (VARA), have also explored or implemented various forms of cross-border cooperation, though often through bilateral agreements or by requiring local presence rather than full passporting. ## What are the key considerations for cross-border recognition?

Key considerations for cross-border recognition include regulatory equivalence, robust AML/CFT standards, consumer protection, data privacy, and operational resilience across all participating jurisdictions. Without alignment on these core principles, mutual recognition becomes challenging.

*   **Regulatory Equivalence**: Jurisdictions must agree that their respective regulatory frameworks achieve comparable outcomes, particularly concerning investor protection and market integrity. This means assessing whether the scope of regulated activities, licensing requirements, and ongoing obligations are sufficiently similar.
*   **AML/CFT Standards**: Adherence to FATF Recommendation 15 and other related standards is paramount. Any jurisdiction seeking cross-border recognition must demonstrate effective implementation of AML/CFT measures, including customer due diligence (CDD) and suspicious transaction reporting (STR) obligations. This is a critical factor shaping Pakistan's virtual asset rules.
*   **Supervisory Cooperation**: Regulators must have mechanisms for effective information sharing and cooperation on supervisory matters, including investigations and enforcement actions. This ensures that a VASP operating across borders remains accountable.
*   **Consumer Protection**: Harmonised or equivalent standards for consumer protection, including complaints handling and client redress requirements, are essential to safeguard users regardless of where the VASP is primarily licensed.
*   **Data Privacy**: Cross-border data flows are inherent to virtual asset services. Agreements on data protection and privacy standards are crucial to ensure compliance with varying national laws.

## What challenges exist for cross-border recognition in Pakistan?

Challenges for Pakistan's cross-border recognition efforts include the nascent stage of its virtual asset regulatory framework, varying levels of regulatory maturity globally, and the need to build trust with international counterparts. Divergent national interests can also complicate harmonisation.

Pakistan's regulatory journey for virtual assets is still evolving. While PVARA is actively working on a comprehensive framework, achieving the level of maturity and international alignment required for widespread passporting agreements takes time. The State Bank of Pakistan's crypto policy and the FBR's view on crypto gains also play roles in the broader landscape that must be considered. Furthermore, the global regulatory landscape for virtual assets remains fragmented, with different jurisdictions adopting diverse approaches to licensing and oversight. This makes it challenging to find common ground for mutual recognition. Building trust among international regulators regarding the robustness and effectiveness of Pakistan's VASP licensing and supervisory regime will be a gradual process.

## What might a future framework look like in Pakistan?

A future framework in Pakistan for cross-border recognition might involve a phased approach, starting with bilateral agreements with jurisdictions that have mature and comparable virtual asset regulations. It could also include a "substance over form" approach, requiring a local presence for significant operations.

Initially, PVARA may focus on establishing its domestic regulatory regime firmly, including clear VASP licence categories explained, capital requirements for virtual asset firms, and robust compliance obligations. As this framework matures, PVARA could explore Memoranda of Understanding (MoUs) or similar agreements with specific countries or economic blocs. These agreements might facilitate a streamlined application process for entities already licensed abroad, rather than full passporting. Such a process could involve:

1.  **Reduced Documentation**: Foreign-licensed entities might submit a simplified application, relying on their home regulator's approval for certain aspects.
2.  **Local Presence Requirement**: Even with recognition, a foreign VASP might still need to establish a local entity or branch in Pakistan to ensure local accountability and compliance with specific Pakistani laws, including those related to the SECP's role in virtual asset regulation.
3.  **Ongoing Supervision**: PVARA would likely retain supervisory oversight over the Pakistani operations of such entities, requiring adherence to local rules on regulatory reporting, market surveillance, and cybersecurity requirements.
