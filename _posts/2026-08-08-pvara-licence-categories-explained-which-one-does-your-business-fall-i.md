## What is PVARA and its mandate?

PVARA, the Pakistan Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority, is the primary body responsible for regulating virtual assets and virtual asset service providers (VASPs) in Pakistan. Its mandate includes developing a comprehensive regulatory framework to ensure market integrity, consumer protection, and the prevention of financial crime within the virtual asset sector. For a detailed guide on the regulator, see our article [What is PVARA? A plain-English guide to Pakistan's virtual asset regulator](/blog/what-is-pvara-pakistan-virtual-assets-regulatory-authority/).

The establishment of PVARA marks a pivotal moment for the virtual asset industry in Pakistan, moving from an unregulated space to one with defined rules and oversight. This initiative aligns with international standards set by bodies like the Financial Action Task Force (FATF), which advocates for robust regulation of virtual assets to combat money laundering and terrorist financing. The State Bank of Pakistan and the Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan (SECP) have also been instrumental in shaping the broader financial regulatory environment within which PVARA operates.

PVARA's responsibilities extend to:
*   Issuing licences to virtual asset service providers.
*   Monitoring compliance with anti-money laundering (AML) and counter-terrorist financing (CTF) regulations.
*   Establishing standards for operational resilience and cybersecurity.
*   Protecting consumers from fraudulent activities and market manipulation.
*   Collaborating with other regulatory bodies like the Federal Board of Revenue (FBR) on tax implications for virtual assets.

## Who needs a PVARA licence?

Any entity or individual engaged in providing virtual asset services to or for another natural or legal person in Pakistan is expected to require a PVARA licence. This broad definition aims to capture a wide range of activities, ensuring comprehensive oversight of the virtual asset sector. Businesses that facilitate virtual asset transactions, custody, or exchange will likely fall under this requirement. For more on this, read our analysis on [Pakistan VASP Licence: Who Needs It and Who Does Not](/blog/who-needs-a-vasp-licence-in-pakistan-and-who-does-not/).

The definition of a Virtual Asset Service Provider (VASP) under PVARA's proposed framework is generally aligned with international standards. It typically includes businesses that conduct activities on behalf of customers such as:

*   **Exchange between virtual assets and fiat currencies:** Facilitating the buying and selling of virtual assets using conventional money.
*   **Exchange between one or more forms of virtual assets:** Allowing users to trade one virtual asset for another.
*   **Transfer of virtual assets:** Moving virtual assets from one address or account to another on behalf of a customer.
*   **Custody and/or administration of virtual assets or instruments enabling control over virtual assets:** Holding or managing virtual assets or private keys for customers.
*   **Participation in and provision of financial services related to an issuer's offer and/or sale of a virtual asset:** This can include initial coin offerings (ICOs) or other fundraising activities involving virtual assets.

It is crucial for businesses to assess their specific activities against PVARA's forthcoming detailed guidelines to confirm if they meet the VASP definition and require a licence. Even if a business does not directly handle customer funds or assets, if its services facilitate the aforementioned activities, it might still be considered a VASP.

## What are the proposed PVARA licence categories?

PVARA is expected to introduce several distinct licence categories, each tailored to specific types of virtual asset services and their associated risk profiles. These categories are designed to ensure proportionate regulation, meaning businesses with higher risk activities face more stringent requirements. The specific categories and their definitions are still under consultation, but general international practice suggests a tiered approach.

While the final categories are subject to PVARA's official pronouncements, common categories observed in other jurisdictions, and likely to be considered by PVARA, include:

1.  **Virtual Asset Exchange Licence:** For platforms that facilitate the trading of virtual assets against fiat currency or other virtual assets. This category typically involves high transaction volumes and liquidity management.
2.  **Virtual Asset Custody Licence:** For entities that hold or manage virtual assets on behalf of customers, including services like cold storage, hot wallets, and key management. This category focuses heavily on security, insurance, and segregation of client assets.
3.  **Virtual Asset Transfer/Remittance Licence:** For businesses that enable the transfer of virtual assets from one party to another, often across borders. This category has strong ties to anti-money laundering (AML) and counter-terrorist financing (CTF) regulations.
4.  **Virtual Asset Brokerage Licence:** For firms that act as intermediaries, matching buyers and sellers of virtual assets without necessarily operating an exchange platform themselves.
5.  **Other Specialised Licences:** PVARA may also introduce categories for specific innovative services, such as those related to decentralised finance (DeFi) or non-fungible tokens (NFTs), as the market evolves.

Each category will come with its own set of capital requirements, operational standards, compliance obligations, and reporting duties. Businesses might also need to obtain multiple licences if they offer a range of services that span different categories.

## How do I determine my business's licence category?

To determine your business's licence category, you must thoroughly analyse all virtual asset services you currently offer or plan to offer, mapping them against PVARA's proposed definitions for each licence type. This involves a detailed review of your operational model, technological infrastructure, and customer interactions. It is essential to consider the primary function of your business and any ancillary services.

A structured approach can help in this assessment:

1.  **List All Services:** Document every virtual asset-related service your business provides. Be comprehensive, from basic exchange functions to more complex custody solutions or advisory roles.
2.  **Review PVARA Definitions:** Carefully study the official descriptions for each proposed licence category as released by PVARA. Pay close attention to keywords and specific activities mentioned. You can find updates on the PVARA website at [https://pvara.org](https://pvara.org).
3.  **Match Services to Categories:** For each service you offer, identify which PVARA licence category it most closely aligns with. Some services might fit into multiple categories, indicating a need for multiple licences or a combined licence type if available.
4.  **Consider the Primary Business Model:** While you may offer various services, identify your core business activity. This often points towards the primary licence category required, with additional licences for secondary activities.
5.  **Seek Clarification:** If there is ambiguity, prepare specific questions for PVARA or consult with regulatory experts. Given the consultation stage, proactive engagement can be beneficial.

## What are the general requirements for a PVARA licence?

General requirements for a PVARA licence are expected to encompass stringent criteria related to capital, governance, operational resilience, and robust anti-money laundering (AML) and counter-terrorist financing (CTF) frameworks. Applicants will need to demonstrate their ability to meet these standards to ensure the safety and soundness of their operations. These requirements are designed to protect consumers and maintain financial stability.

While specific figures and detailed policies are awaiting finalisation, based on international best practices and the stated goals of PVARA, applicants should prepare for requirements that typically include:

1.  **Minimum Capital Requirements:** Businesses will likely need to maintain a certain level of paid-up capital or net tangible assets to demonstrate financial stability. The exact amount will vary by licence category.
2.  **Fit and Proper Criteria:** Key personnel, including directors, senior management, and significant shareholders, will undergo checks to ensure they have the necessary competence, integrity, and financial soundness.
3.  **Robust Governance and Risk Management:** A clear organisational structure, comprehensive internal controls, and effective risk management policies (operational, technological, financial, legal) will be mandatory.
4.  **AML/CTF Framework:** Implementation of a strong AML/CTF programme, including:
    *   Customer Due Diligence (CDD) and Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD) procedures.
    *   Transaction monitoring systems.
    *   Suspicious Transaction Reporting (STR) mechanisms to the Financial Monitoring Unit (FMU).
    *   Appointment of a qualified Compliance Officer.
5.  **Cybersecurity and Data Protection:** Robust measures to protect customer data and virtual assets from cyber threats, including regular audits and incident response plans.
6.  **Operational Resilience:** Plans for business continuity and disaster recovery to ensure uninterrupted service delivery.
7.  **Consumer Protection Measures:** Clear terms and conditions, transparent fee structures, and effective complaint handling mechanisms.
8.  **Physical Presence:** Depending on the licence, a physical office in Pakistan may be required.
9.  **Technology Audit:** An independent audit of the technology infrastructure and security protocols may be necessary.

Meeting these requirements will involve significant investment in compliance infrastructure and expertise.
